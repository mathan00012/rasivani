Not Pythagoras? Baudhayana's Theorem and √2 with a Rope

The Baudhayana Shulba Sutra states a² + b² = c² as a rule of rope for building fire altars. An honest timeline of Babylon, Baudhayana, Pythagoras and Euclid, and a value of √2 correct to five decimal places.

The full story

Every student learns Pythagoras' theorem. But an Indian text stated it early, to build fire altars. Who was first? Let us look honestly.

The rule of the rope

Is Pythagoras' theorem really Indian? Between about the eighth and sixth centuries BCE, priests wrote rules for building fire altars: the Shulba Sutras. Baudhayana writes: the diagonal rope of a rectangle makes an area equal to both of its sides' areas together. That is the theorem: a squared plus b squared equals c squared, written in India, most likely before Pythagoras.

Who came first? The honest answer

To be honest, the Babylonians wrote such number triples on clay tablets by around eighteen hundred BCE. Baudhayana stated it as a general rule, and Euclid later wrote a proof. No single nation owns this idea.

The square root of two

The Shulba Sutras hide a gem: the square root of two. To double a square altar, Baudhayana gives a recipe: one, plus a third, plus a twelfth, minus a tiny part. The answer is one point four one four two one five, correct to five decimal places, using only rope and pegs. The care to build an altar exactly right is what gave birth to geometry in India.
