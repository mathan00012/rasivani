Binary Code 2,000 Years Before Computers? Zero and Pingala's Secret

Computers run on 0 and 1. More than 2,000 years ago, the Indian scholar Pingala counted the short and long syllables of poetry in the same way. Brahmagupta's rules for zero, the dot-zero of the Bakhshali manuscript, the Gwalior temple inscription, and the 'Fibonacci' numbers an Indian poet found centuries earlier.

The full story

Zero and one. Every computer and phone today runs on just these two digits. Yet more than two thousand years ago, an Indian poet wrote down the same kind of counting. For poetry!

Short and long: just two kinds

His name was Pingala, and his book, the Chandah Shastra, is about the rhythm of verse. Sanskrit verse has only two kinds of syllables: short, called laghu, and long, called guru. How many ways can you arrange three syllables? Pingala listed them all, in order. Exactly eight. He also gave a method to turn each pattern into a number. Read guru as zero and laghu as one, and that is binary!

How zero was born

Now, zero. Many civilisations had a mark for an empty place. But the first to treat zero as a real number, with rules to add and subtract it, was Brahmagupta, in the year 628. A number minus itself is zero. Simple today. Revolutionary then.

Zero written as a dot

In an old maths manuscript called the Bakhshali manuscript, now in Oxford, zero is written as a small dot. At a small temple in Gwalior, a stone inscription shows the round zero we write today. It is about 1,150 years old. These numerals reached Europe through the Arab world. Arab scholars called them the Indian numerals.

Who came first?

One more surprise. A poet named Virahanka, counting verse patterns, found the series 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13. The world calls them Fibonacci numbers. But they were known in India centuries before Fibonacci. Leibniz published binary in Europe in 1703. Pingala did not invent the computer, but the counting idea is far older here.
