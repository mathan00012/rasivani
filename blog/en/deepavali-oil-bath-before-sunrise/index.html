Why Oil Bath Before Sunrise on Deepavali? The Secret of Lamps on the Darkest Night

Why do millions of Indians take an oil bath before sunrise on Deepavali, and ask "Ganga snanam aacha?" Why are lamps lit on Amavasya, the darkest night of the month? The story of Narakasura, the Ayurveda reason behind the oil bath, how North India, Bengal, Gujarat, Jains and Sikhs celebrate, and the truth about the viral "NASA Diwali photo".

The full story

Oil bath. New clothes. Sweets. Lamps. This is how Deepavali begins in millions of homes. But why an oil bath before sunrise? And why lamps on the darkest night of the month?

The story of Narakasura

In Tamil Nadu, Deepavali is Naraka Chaturdashi, the fourteenth day of the waning moon. Tradition says that on this day, Krishna, with Satyabhama, defeated the tyrant Narakasura. His last wish, the story goes, was that people remember the day with lights, not tears.

Why the oil bath at dawn

So the day starts before sunrise, with sesame oil on the head and a warm bath. In Tamil homes, everyone asks the same question that morning: Ganga snanam aacha? Have you had your Ganga bath? An old saying explains it. On this dawn, Lakshmi lives in the oil, and Ganga lives in the water.

Tradition and science

Ayurveda adds a practical reason. Deepavali comes as the cool, dry season begins. A warm oil massage, called abhyanga, keeps skin and joints from drying out. So the ritual and the season agree.

Why lamps on a new-moon night

Now the lamps. Deepavali comes at Amavasya, the new moon. It is the darkest night of the month. And that is the point. The word itself means a row of lamps: deepa, a lamp, and avali, a row. On the darkest night, every home lights a row of lamps. We choose light exactly when the dark is deepest.

One festival, many stories

Travel north and the story changes. There, Deepavali marks Rama's return to Ayodhya, with Lakshmi Puja at night. Bengal worships Kali. Gujarat begins a new year. Jains remember Mahavira, and Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas. Many stories, one idea: light wins over darkness.

That viral 'NASA photo'

Every year, a colourful 'NASA photo of India on Deepavali night' goes viral. Here is the truth. That image is a map of how city lights grew over many years. It is not one festival night. Our lamps are real, but that photo is not. So check before you forward!

A kinder, safer Deepavali

Before you celebrate, four small things make the day kinder. Light at least one oil lamp. Keep a bucket of water near the crackers. Think of pets, birds and elderly neighbours. And share sweets with someone who works for you all year.
